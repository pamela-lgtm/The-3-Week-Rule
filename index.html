One calculation every rental owner should know

How long can you afford
to hold out?

There is an exact answer to that question, it takes ten seconds to work out, and it comes out almost the same for every rental property in Central Florida. Here it is.

The rule

Break-even, in days
365 ×
the percentage you are reaching for
For almost every home
~3 weeks
Hold out longer than that and the higher rent has cost you money

Reaching for 6% more rent? You have 22 days. Reaching for 7%? 25 days. That is the entire budget — and it applies whether your home rents for $1,500 or $6,000 a month.

The question this answers

You are setting a rent. One number is what the market is clearly paying. The other is a bit more — the number you would like to get, and might.

Asking for more is not unreasonable. It might work. The honest question is simply this: how long can you afford to find out?

Because the extra rent only arrives if someone signs a lease. The vacancy starts costing you immediately.

Most owners have never put a number on that trade. It turns out to be a very precise one.

Worked all the way through

A home the market will pay $2,900 for. You would like $3,100.

StepResult
The extra rent you are hoping for   $3,100 − $2,900$200 per month
Multiply by 12 — the length of a standard lease   $200 × 12$2,400
What one vacant day costs   $3,100 ÷ 30$103 per day
Divide one by the other   $2,400 ÷ $10323 days

Twenty-three days. That is the whole budget. If asking $3,100 instead of $2,900 costs you more than 23 extra days of vacancy, you have lost money — and you will still have lost it even if a renter eventually pays the $3,100.

Put the other way round: the extra $200 a month has to survive eleven months of tenancy just to pay back three and a half weeks of an empty house.

Why twelve months?

Because that is how long you actually get to collect the higher rent. A standard long-term lease runs twelve months, so the extra $200 is not $200 forever — it is $200 for twelve payments, and then the rent resets at renewal. Using anything longer than twelve flatters the decision.

Now the surprising part

You would expect a $6,000 home to have a very different answer from a $1,500 home. It does not.

Break-even days = 365 × (the extra rent ÷ the rent you are asking)

In plain terms: multiply 365 by the percentage you are reaching for.

The reason is that both halves of the calculation scale together. A higher rent produces a bigger annual gain — and a bigger daily vacancy cost. They cancel each other out. What is left is the percentage you are reaching for, and nothing else.

And it holds exactly when the percentage is identical. A 6.2% stretch breaks even at 23 days whether the home rents for $1,500, $3,000 or $6,000 a month.

Roughly three weeks

Because almost nobody reaches by more than 5 to 8%, the honest answer for nearly every rental home is the same. If asking more costs you more than about three weeks of vacancy, you have lost money.

So your real target is two and a half weeks

Break-even is not the goal. Break-even means you did all of that for nothing — you took the risk, waited out the vacancy, and ended exactly where you would have been by pricing correctly on day one.

To actually come out ahead, you have to lease well inside the line.

Seventeen days. Two and a half weeks. That is the window in which a higher asking price actually earns you something.

It is also why we measure a new listing at day five rather than waiting a month to see what happens. On a 23-day budget, five days is already a fifth of it spent — and by the time a home has been sitting a month, the decision has been made for you.

Run it on your own property

Thirty seconds, and most owners have never done it.
